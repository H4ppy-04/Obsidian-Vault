#### Safety
The risk of dying while hiking is generally very low, although it varies considerably depending on the environment, activity, remoteness, weather, and individual preparation. Fatal incidents can occur through falls, heat-related illness, medical emergencies, exposure, and getting lost.

Fatality statistics should be interpreted carefully, as studies may measure different populations and types of outdoor activity. A reported fatality rate should therefore be understood in the context of its methodology rather than treated as a universal probability for every hiker.

In Australia the main causes of fatalities are:
- Falls (Queensland's [Glass House Mountains](https://www.australia.com/en/places/brisbane-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-glass-house-mountains.html), [Federation Peak](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/australia/tasmania/federation-peak) in Tasmania)
- Dehydration where temperatures exceed 40 degrees in some places like[ Larapinta Trail](https://www.larapintatrail.com.au/) or [Mount Augustus](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/mount-augustus-national-park)
- Medical emergencies and exhaustion. This includes things like [cardiac events](https://www.hri.org.au/health/learn/cardiovascular-disease/the-12-most-common-heart-and-cardiovascular-conditions) or physically collapsing from extreme exertion where you are cutoff from civilization and unable to get help.
- Getting lost (which then can lead to exposure or hypothermia)

It should be noted that your likelihood of actually dying when hiking is **extremely low** but not impossible so you should always be prepared and vigilant when engaging in any outdoor activity that can result in death.

Do not light fires in non-designated zones. Wildfires affect Australia in massive ways. They impact the environment, peoples homes and take real human lives. Accidentally starting a wildfire that gets out of control is extremely easy to do, and therefore you should try and only cook food on open flames in designated fire zones. Otherwise, stick to dry food as you do not *need* to have cooked food on a hike, although it can make it a better experience.

Do not physically touch, provoke, or attempt to handle wildlife. Most wildlife will generally avoid humans, but animals can behave defensively when threatened, surprised, or protecting offspring. If you encounter a snake or spider, maintain a safe distance, avoid disturbing it, and allow it to move away. Be mindful of where you step, particularly when walking through dense vegetation or rocky terrain. Staying on marked trails can reduce exposure to hazards, but it does not eliminate the risk.

##### Preparation
When preparing you should always tell at least one person where you are going. This is so that in the off chance you go missing, they know roughly where to find you. You should always take enough food and water. If you do not have enough room for water or do not want to overpack, ensure that there are hydration stops on the way. This can be done by sourcing local guides from the council or looking at hiking apps such as [AllTrails](https://www.alltrails.com) or [trailhiking.com](https://www.trailhiking.com.au/). It's also important that you consider that not all water stops are reliable to drink from. Consider the conditions of the season as well as water treatment. Never drink unfiltered water from an open water source like a stream or a pond as they can contain bacteria like *E. Coli* or parasites like Giardia that lead to [Giardiasis](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/giardia-infection/symptoms-causes/syc-20372786). 

Considering the weather is also important. Hiking in extreme heat is very dangerous and not recommended. This is for many reasons but the main ones are that it's easier to make poorer decisions, underestimating the sheer amount of water that you need to replace in your body over time, and the increased risk of conditions like heat exhaustion or even heat stroke. In the same way, hiking in cold weather need sufficient preparation as well, ensuring that you have clothes that can accommodate the temperature. This also applies to equipment. Ensure things like your sleeping bag have a temperature rating similar to the environment you plan to be camping in.

Perhaps contrary to popular belief, you do not need to have the "best gear" or the "top of the shelf equipment" in order to have a fun and comfortable experience. By not over or under-packing, and ensuring that you pick the appropriate items to carry and clothes to wear and food to eat, you will have a fine experience. The difficulty comes when you forget essential things like food, spare clothes, first aid equipment, tracking equipment, and so on. More expensive or dedicated hiking gear can improve your experience, but I would sooner have the knowledge of a map and compass than a pair of $500 hiking shoes as those shoes will do you no good if you're lost and starving. This is where it comes to knowing more about how to use the gear you have then purchasing expensive gear to accommodate for a lack of knowledge.
