The church has had quite the relationship with slavery, at times condemning it and then at times permitting it. If there would be any evidence for moral relativism it would be found by looking at cases where the church has changed in moral opinion mostly depending on the time period and on the cultural customs that existed in society at that time as well. 

The early Christian church certainly didn't mind it at all. There is plenty of evidence that Christian communities at that time continued to contain both slaves and slaveholders. There was no evidence of abolition at all, and it was accepted as a social institution. 

In Late Antiquity and the following Middle ages the church continued to have a relationship to slavery, and there was still no abolition, even with thinkers like Thomas Aquinas regarding some forms of slavery as justified. 

Then in the 15th-16th centuries there were some papal documents that explicitly authorized the enslavement of non-Christians especially as the Europeans were expanding at that time into Africa. (see Pope Nicholas V's Dum Diversas 1452) as well as his Romanus Pontifex in 1455. 

But then you have in the next century in the 1500's now saying that you shouldn't enslave Indigenous Americans.

Then even with the Atlantic slave trade you had Christians on both sides. In fact it was only in the 18th and 19th centuries where you truly had a consensus among Christians. But all throughout history the most pro-slavery arguments came largely from Christians who were arguing from both natural theology as well as the bible. 

|Period|Broad Christian relationship with slavery|
|---|---|
|**Early Church**|Tolerated existing slavery; emphasised spiritual equality and humane treatment|
|**Late Antiquity–Medieval**|Increasing moral criticism/restrictions, but slavery remained accepted in various forms|
|**15th century**|Some papal documents explicitly authorised enslavement of non-Christians|
|**16th–17th centuries**|Increasing theological disputes and condemnations of particular forms of slavery, while Christians continued participating in it|
|**18th century**|Quakers and other Christians increasingly develop explicit anti-slavery positions|
|**Late 18th–19th century**|Evangelical/Quaker abolitionism becomes a major political force|
|**19th century**|Christianity becomes both a major justification for **and** opponent of slavery, especially in America|
|**Modern Christianity**|Slavery is overwhelmingly regarded by Christian churches as morally abhorrent|
