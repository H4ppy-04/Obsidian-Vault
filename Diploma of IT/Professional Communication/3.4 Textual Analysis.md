As a critical reader you need to be able to develop your own ideas about what you read. 

Critical analysis is the process of: 

- Examining 
    
- Interpreting 
    
- Evaluating texts 
    

Evaluate arguments and form a reasoned judgement at the end. Critical reading in conjunction with reflection. 

- Breaking down the content in components 
    
- Question the assumptions 
    
- Identifying biases (if there are any) 
    
- Considering the context in which it was created. 
    

Key components of textual analysis: 

- Understand the text fully comprehending the main ideas, evidence and so forth. 
    
- Identify key arguments, recognizing the primary claims and supporting evidence. 
    
- Evaluate the evidence, assessing the credibility, relevance and sufficiency. 
    
- Considering context. Understanding historical, cultural and situational context of the text. 
    
- Questioning assumptions  - identify underlying assumptions and biases 
    
- Form a judgement at the end. Develop reasoning, interpretations or critique based on the analysis. 
    

In academic contexts. 

- Provide informed reasoning backed by evidence and ideas from trustworthy sources such as books and academic articles 
    
- Identify context, background author bias that may lead to distortion within what you read and hear. 
    
- Identify and question unfounded assumptions. 
    
- Explain the significance and consequences of particular data arguments and conclusions made by others. 
    

Textual analysis is important because it: 

- Enhances understanding. Critical analysis helps students gain a deeper understanding of complex texts, and ideas and allowed them to engage more thoughtfully with material. 
    
- Develops critical thinking skills. Encourages students to question assumptions and formed reasoned judgements. 
    
- Improves academic writing. By practicing critical analysis students can articulate their ideas persuasively. 
    
- It promotes independent thought. 
    
- Prepares for advanced research because it involves evaluating sources, synthesizing information and developing original insight. 
    

Critical analysis is also useful for professional contexts as it: 

- Helps with making informed decisions by evaluating information, identifying biases, considering multiple perspectives. 
    
- Helps with problem solving, it encourages thorough examinations of issues and development of well-reasoned solutions.  
    
- Leads to effective communication – better equipped to communicate their ideas clearly and effectively. 
    
- Helps with adaptability and adapt to changing circumstances. 
    
- Ethical considerations – it encourages ethical decision making through examination of the consequences of actions. 
    

Process of Textual Analysis 

- Read the text carefully and have a thorough understanding of the text. 
    
- Identify the author's thesis or argument. Usually found in the introduction or conclusion. 
    
- Examine the structure of the text. 
    
- Identify key themes 
    
- Critique the arguments. 
    
- Draw conclusions 
    

Evaluate the evidence 

- Think beyond the text and do research 
    
- Identify gaps and biases 
    
- Selective inclusion of evidence that only supports a certain view. 
    
- Words that indicate the author is overstating or making unjustified assumptions, e.g. (obviously, plainly, naturally, etc.) 
    
- Overgeneralizations and assumptions: 
    
    - Researches make simplified assumptions when tackling a complex problem. 
        

Methodological Limitations 

- There almost always happen with studies. 
    
- This isn't necessarily a problem as long as these limitations are acknowledged. 
    
- Limitations due to sample group. 
    

Power refers to how strong the evidence is. 

Objectivity of Research 

- Some researched may be biased in structure. 
    
- Correlation vs Causation 
    

Questions to ask when critically analyzing information: 

- What do I already know? What do I need to work out? 
    
- Is this fact or opinion? What evidence is used to back their claim? 
    
- What are the stated and unstated assumptions in this information? Are there any other ways we can think about this? Is it convincing and relevant?
