About this lesson 

This lesson teaches students how to research different sources, outline the difference between different sources, categorize sources, SEO, determining if sources are reliable and how to reference properly. 

Research Skills 

- Authority: Use peer-reviews journals, professional publications, official government sites, internal orgs, universities, and books from reputable authors or publishers 
    
- Current: Ensure that information is up to date. It depends on the field (fields that are rapidly changing may be not as current) 
    
- Objectivity: Minimize bias as much as possible. Avoid sources funded by interested parties or used for advertising.  
    
- Audience: Prefer materials produced for academic or professional audiences over those for popular audiences.  
    
    - Texts for general audiences over "illustrate and explain" issues rather than critically analyze them. 
        
- Transparency: Ensure clarity and accuracy in information, explaining methods of data analysis and collection.

Activity 1 – Evaluating Sources. 

Galois, Wikipedia Contributors (2026) is quite accurate pertaining to the authority outlined in the ACOAT criteria as errors get corrected quite quickly due to high exposure and accessibility. It is classified as a tertiary source. Moreover, The article is also generally current, as it's last modification was in March. Whilst not immune from bias, articles are generally objective as sources must be linked and the policy of the organization states that authors must strive to be neutral. Galois, Wikipedia Contributors (2026) has clear and accurate information, however it does not meet the criteria of being a reliable source. As reliable sources typically are oriented towards academic or professional audiences, Galois, Wikipedia Contributors (2026) does not meet that criteria. Furthermore, edits are anonymous and lack any identifiable expert author, rendering the source unfit and unreliable for referencing to a professional or academic standard.  

The Britannica article on the French mathematician Évariste Galois, (Encyclopedia Britannica, 2026), is a tertiary source as it compiles information from primary and secondary sources. Galois, (Encyclopedia Britannica, 2026) is highly accurate as its authors are identifiable experts and there is a formal review process for any modification to the article that affects its content. The article is current as its most recent edit was in 2026, and it refers to a historical figure, not a current event, making it especially current. Britannica as a source in general is educational and not persuasive, whilst also attempting to make its information as objective as possible. It caters to all audiences and is highly transparent due to its editorial standards and reputation as a source. However, it should be noted that convention states that encyclopedias are not reliable sources anymore and on that basis the criteria can generally be overruled.  Britannica is written not exclusively for an academic audience. In conclusion, Galois, (Encyclopedia Britannica, 2026) stands as a non-reliable source and ineligible for use in professional and academic work. 

The book "The French Mathematician" by Tom Petsinis is considered a secondary, non-scholarly source, as a piece of literature aimed to be written for storytelling purposes. The author, Tom Petsinis, whilst being a respected writer and academic is not a historian or mathematician, nor someone who specializes academically in Galois, meaning that there are more respected sources that could be referenced. However, that does not mean that this source isn't of value, it just means that the author is not an expert in this field so the work doesn't carry as much weight in its authority. The book was also published in 1997 so it's relatively outdated, especially compared with more contemporary works. Petsinis, (1997) is also an interpretive biography, so certain aspects of the text may lack evidence. The audience of this text is readers interested in historical fiction or biographies, and perhaps those interested in the history of mathematics, not professionals or academics. The text (Petsinis, 1997) is also not fully transparent as it doesn't distinguish between evidence and interpretation as it is an interpretive biography. Overall, Petsinis (1997) is not highly reliable as an academic text for historical work as it lacks evidence and transparency.  

Sarton (1921) is a secondary source, specifically a biographical essay seemingly published in a science magazine. It has strong authorship as Dr. George Sarton is (according to Britannica) a scholar who helped make the history of science a legitimate academic discipline. However, the age of the source is quite old, over 100 years, meaning that newer scholarship could supersede this source. The objectivity of Sarton (1921) seems to be somewhat credible. The abstract reads to be somewhat admiring of Galois, so it doesn't seem to be very detached like a report would be, for example. Furthermore, given that it's published in a science magazine, it's audience seems to be those who specialize or have an academic of professional interest in this field, as well as specialists. Sarton (1921) is fairly transparent, but most biographical facts are borrowed from an external biography. Overall, this source is reliable if you are going to talk about how Galois was perceived in early scholarship.  

Radloff (2002) is a scholarly secondary source. It has strong authorship as the author is named, and it appears in a specialized journal. Radloff (2002) is moderate in its contemporary standing, being published in 2002, as with previously mentioned sources it may be superseded by more recent articles. In its objectivity its fairly strong as Radloff (2002) is explanatory rather than a historical document. The article also has decent transparency as all of its properties (DOI, author, journal, etc.) are named. I would be personally comfortable using this source in a university assignment.
