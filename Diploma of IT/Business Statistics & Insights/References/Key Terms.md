**Sample**: (As opposed to a population) $\to$ a subset of broader data.
**Target population**: A well-defined group that comprises all relevant subjects of interest.
**Representative sample**: A ==random== sample that shares similar characteristics with the ==target population==. It should be sufficiently large to give accurate information.
**Variable**: A variable is a measurement that is taken from any study that is conducted. Examples may include gender, age or income.
**Experimental study**: A study where the investigator has some level of control over the ==intervention==.
**Observational study:** A study where there is **no intervention** by the investigator, *nor* is there any treatment imposed.[^1]
**Random sample:** A sample where each member of the target population has an *equal chance* to be selected.[^2]
**Simple random sample:** ==specifically requires that _every possible group_ of a given size has an equal and independent probability of being chosen==.
**Mode**: the most frequent observation (category that has the highest count).
**Median**: the middle element in a set.
**Mean**: the average of all elements in the set.
**Null Hypothesis $H_0$**: The baseline assumption in an experiment that **there is no real effect, no difference, or no relationship** between the variables being studied.
**Significance level $\alpha$** : The threshold or probability cutoff set by researchers _before_ running a study to decide when a result is strong enough to reject the null hypothesis.
**Confounder/Confounding variable**: An unmeasured or extraneous variable that is associated with **both** the cause (independent variable) and the outcome (dependent variable) being studied.
**Confounding bias**: The distortion or systematic error that occurs in an estimate of an effect when a confounder is present and not properly controlled or accounted for.
 **Type $I$ error**: ==a [false positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_and_type_II_errors) where you incorrectly reject a true null hypothesis==
 **P-value**: ==a statistical measure that tells you the chance of getting your test results, or more extreme results, if the null hypothesis is true.==
 

[^1]: Examples would be something where you don't have the ability to change any factor during the study.
[^2]: An way to get a random sample may be to do an *in-person* sample, like going to a cafe or the student library. Alternatively, one might enumerate between every n$^{\text{th}}$ student within the total population.
