### Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states that:
>[!quote]
>The mean of a random sample has a sampling distribution whose shape can be approximated by a Normal distribution. The larger the sample, the better the approximation will be.
>
> From Business Statistics (Global edition) p.334

The closer to the *original population* is to a Normal distribution, the *smaller* the sample size required for the CLT effect to apply. An $n$ value of **at least 25** will be 'large enough' to assume an approximate Normal distribution for sample means.



However, if the original population *is* a Normal distribution, then the sample means will follow an exact Normal distribution *regardless* of the $n$ value. So we don't need the CLT.

![[modes of histograms.png]]

If you have a distribution skewed left or skewed right you want to look at the **median**. 
Most distributions are normal so we look at the mean most of the time.

>[!tip]
>We are interested in the mean for **two reasons**:
>1. We have a [[1.2 Categorizing and Presenting Variables|Numerical Variable]]
>2. We have a Normal distribution

### General Rule:
**CLT:** As sample size increases, the larger the standard deviation. The histograms also become more Normal.  This is shown in these cases, where as the sample increase, there is less spread and visually things become more concatenated. Furthermore, spread should be reduced in statistically analysis when possible. 
![[histograms according to CLT.png]]


### Standard Error
The formula for the standard error is:

$$SE={\sigma\over\sqrt{n}}$$
The **standard error is the difference between population mean $\mu$ and the sample mean $\bar{y}$**. The goal is to <u>reduce</u> the standard error value. The larger the $SE$, the less accurate the sample becomes. Always attempt to reduce the value of the standard error. This is done by *increasing* the sample size because the larger the denominator the smaller the value of the fraction.

>[!tip]
 By CLT if a sample $\ge 25$ then we assume a Normal distribution.[^1]

 - The center of the distribution of sample means remained close to the population mean.
 - The spread in the distribution of sample means decreased.
 - The shape of the distribution of sample means became closer to a Normal distribution.

### Hypothesis Testing

>[!definition]
>A **hypothesis test** is a process involving a series of formal steps.

**Steps**:
1. State the null and the alternative hypotheses in terms of the parameter of interest.
2. Check the underlying assumptions of the test.
3. Calculate the test statistic.
4. Obtain the P-value for the test from the distribution of the test statistic.
5. If the P-value is less than 0.05 (the significance level), reject the null hypothesis. If the P-value is not less than 0.05, do not reject the null hypothesis.
6. Write a conclusion to the original research question in terms of the target population.

A **null hypothesis** is a statement that a population parameter has a known or specified  (claimed) value: $$H_0:\mu=\mu_0$$
The **alternative hypothesis** is a statement that the **null hypothesis** is **not true**. Depending on the **comparison operator** this informs if we should do a **one tailed** or **two-tailed** test. For instance if $$H_a:\mu\gt \mu_0$$ then this would be a **one-tailed** test, however, if $H_a$ was $$H_a:\mu \neq \mu_0$$ then this would be a **two-tailed** test. (there are two possibilities).


**Rules to follow when doing hypotheses**
1. Always have the **population statistics** go into the hypothesis where $\mu$ is the population mean and $p$ is the **population proportion**. $\beta$ is the **population slope**. 
2. You always have the $=$ sign in $H_0$ and the $\neq$ sign in $H_a$
3. ==Always have the **same value** in both the **null** and **alternative** hypotheses.== This is because they should be **mutually exclusive**.

**How to test the normality assumption**:[^2]
1. The **question** will tell you that the **distribution** is Normal, in which case you don't have to check it's Normal.
2. The question will give you a [[1.4 Histograms|Histogram]], in which case you will need to look at the shape and **comment on the shape**. In most cases, it will be **normal**. When commenting on the shape of the histogram, **loosen the criteria**: it doesn't necessarily have to be **completely symmetric.**
3. Check the **Central Limit Theorem**. ($n\ge25$) 

>[!warning]
>There are edge cases. For instance, when there is a histogram that is heavily skewed and $n=100$. This is as per the central limit theorem, even though it is heavily skewed. The CLT will override this assumption.

**Test statistic**
A formula that is applied to calculate a $t$ value. This is called a $t$-test. This will be covered in tomorrows lesson.

#### P value
This can stand for:
1. Probability
2. Proportion
3. (or) Percentage

>[!note]
>Always convert Proportion or Percentage into Probability form.
>
>To do this you divide the numerator divided by the denominator, to get the probability. Proportion is seldom used, but percentage is often used in this unit. 
>
>To get the percentage into probability you just do $x \div 100$ and that gets the probability where $x$ is the percentage.

The $p$-value is (commonly) the probability. 

>[!definition] **p-value**
>The $p$-value is the probability of the null hypothesis $H_0$ to be **true** or **reasonable**.

Example:
$H_0:\mu=350$
$P=0.93$ (93\% chance that $H_0$ is true)

Consider however,
$H_0:\mu=\$3,000$
$P=0.002$ (Reject $H_0$) since it's below $\alpha=0.05$

>[!tip]
>In the final exam, the $p$-value will be given in the form of an **output table**. This will require the calculation of $df$ (degrees of freedom) which is just $n-1$.

**Format of the Conclusion**:

- Relay back to the scenario
- State whether we can reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis
- State the entailment as it relates back to the **research question**
