>[!definition] 
>A **measure of centre** tells us where we can find the ‘middle’ of the data.


>[!definition]
>The **mean** is defined as the arithmetic average.
>$$
>\bar{y} = \frac{y_1 + y_2+\ldots\ldots\ldots+y_n}{n}
>$$


>[!definition]
>**Standard deviation** is defined in terms of the derivations of data from the mean (*residuals*). It is the square root of the average squared residual.
>
>$$
>s = \sqrt{\frac{(y_1-\bar{y})^2+(y_2-\bar{y})^2+\ldots\ldots+(y_n-\bar{y})^2}{n-1}}
>$$
>The *sample* standard deviation $s$ estimates the population standard deviation, $\sigma$. The variance, $\sigma^2$ is the square of the standard deviation and is measured by $s^2$


**Median** is the value that is *less likely* to be affected by extreme values in the data-set. It's more **robust** because the median doesn't have to consider outliers.

>[!definition]
>**Outlier** is a value that is *significantly different* to the rest of the data set. There can be more than one outlier.

### Summarizing ==One numerical variable==

 - **Measure of centre.** 
	 - Why?
		- The ==reason== why we want to measure the centre is because it allows us to know where the "central tendency" is. Measures of "central tendency" include:
	 - Mean which can be represented as $\bar{y}$
	 - Median which is the middle value of a set $[1, 3, 7, 9, 10]$ so the middle would be $7$. It should be arranged in ascending order always. 
 - **Measure of spread:** Tells us how spread out the data is.
	 - Why?
		 - This can be helpful to know because you might think the sample observations are very similar but 49, 50, 51 and 0, 50, 100 would have the same mean, but are radically different observations.
		 - ==Only knowing the "measure of centre" will not give you the entire context. You also need to know the **measure of spread**==.
	 - Standard deviation (SD). The total difference of all of the dots, where you draw a line from the difference of an observation to the mean.
		 - The smaller the standard deviation the better the estimate.
			 - There is less variability in the data which means more certainty.
		 - $s$ indicates the SD (standard deviation). 
	 - Range. $Range = max - min$ This is just the difference between the max and min in the set. 
	 - IQR. Inter-quotile range This is the upper quotile (quotile 3) - lower quotile (quotile 1). This is also more **robust**; It's less likely to be affected by significantly high and significantly low values.
		 - Lower quotile - the cutoff value for the smallest (25%) of all the observations in a sample
		 - Upper quotile - the cutoff value for the lowest (75%) - can also be articulated as the top (25%) of all of the observations in a sample.
		 - IQR is the middle 50%. 
 - **Graphical displays**
	 - Histogram
	 - Box plot. The full name is a **box of whiskers plot**
		 - Majority will be vertical
		 - Lower quartile: The bottom 25% 
		 - Upper quartile: The top 25%

#### How to Determine Outliers

1. Identify lower fence and upper fence
2. $$IQR=UQ-LQ$$
3. Calculate the lower fence and upper fence$$LF=LQ-1.5\times IQR$$$$UF=UQ+1.5\times IQR$$
4. If an outlier is above or below these fences then it's an outlier.
