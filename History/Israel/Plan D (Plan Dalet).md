Plan D (also known as Plan Dalet) was a Zionist military plan (commanded by the primary national founder and the first Prime Minister of Israel) that to place during the 1948 Palestine war for the conquest of territory in Mandatory Palestine (Khalidi, 1988). The plan itself described the beginning of a "new phase" in the 1948 Israel-Palestine war in which Zionist forces shifted to the offensive.

Crucially, Plan Dalet was drafter **after** the [[1947 Partition Plan]] and during the [[1948 Arab-Israeli War]]. It was a plan to gain control of and defend the territory allotted to the Jewish state as per the [[1947 Partition Plan]]. 

The *first conception* or hinting of this idea to conquest Palestine was in 1937 when the commander of the forces in the Tel-Aviv area (named Avnir) received an order from Ben-Gurion according to the official history of the Haganah. Anticipating an eventual British withdrawal after the Peel Report (Palestinian Royal Commission by the British to investigate the causes of conflict in Mandatory Palestine)

Plan Dalet was the *fourth iteration or "adjustment"* of previous plans:
- Plan Aleph (Plan A) (Feb, 1945)
- Plan Bet (Plan B) (Sep, 1945)
- Plan Gimel (Plan C) (May, 1946)
- Plan Dalet (Plan D) (March, 1948)

The **guidelines** were:
1. Take control of Mandatory Palestine
2. Declare a Jewish state
3. Defend its borders and people (including the Jewish population outside of the borders "before, and in anticipation of" the invasion by regular Arab armies. )
It specifically included gaining control of areas where Yishuv (see [[Historical Israel]]) populations existed, including those outside the borders of the *proposed* Jewish state.

Tactics included:
1. Lay siege to Palestinian-Arab villages
2. Bombing neighborhoods of cities
3. Forced expulsion of their inhabitants
4. Settings fields and houses on fire and detonating TNT in the rubble to "prevent any return".
5. Zionist military units also possessed detailed lists of neighborhoods and villages to be destroyed and the Arab inhabitants expelled.

One of the most well known operations of Plan Dalet (Plan D) was the Deir Yassin massacre. 


Why it's contentious, specifically regarding the intent, with some historians asserting it was *defensive* and others stating that it was aimed at expulsion and conquest:
1. The plan itself outlined several alarming ways by which the operation would be carried out, including the bombing and setting fire to of villages.
2. It involved extreme violence.

Furthermore, historians that assert the plan was aimed at maximum conquest and expulsion include Walid Khalidi:

>[!quote] Address to the [American Committee on Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Task_Force_on_Palestine):
>"As is witnessed by the Haganah's Plan Dalet, the Jewish leadership was determined to link the envisaged Jewish state with the Jerusalem _corpus separatum_. But the corpus separatum lay deep in Arab territory, in the middle of the envisaged Palestinian state, so this linking up could only be done militarily."

The strongest argument in favor of the idea that the *intent* of the Jewish leadership to maximize expulsion and conquest is able to be re-stated as follows:
1. The Haganah[^1] iteration of Plan Dalet determined to fulfill an envisaged ideal of the Jewish state.
2. The corpus separatum lay deep in Arab territory
3. Conclusion: The only way to link Jerusalem corpus separatum with the envisaged Jewish state was to do this through military conquest of surround Arab nations.

However, Yoav Gelber, an Israeli historian, holds that it was a defensive operation with the goals of:
1. Protecting the borders of the upcoming Jewish state according to the partition line;
2. Securing its territorial continuity in the face of invasion attempts
3. Safeguarding freedom of movement on the roads
4. Enabling continuation of essential daily routines
He also rejects what he calls the "Palestinian-invented" version of Plan Dalet. He says, crucially: 

>[!quote] Yoav Gelber, Israeli Historian
>"The text clarified unequivocally that expulsion concerned only those villages that would fight against the Hagana and resist occupation, and not all Arab hamlets" (Gelber, 2001)
