
Aquinas' Five Ways

## Why they suck as *"proofs"* for god

Aquinas’s _Five Ways_ don’t hold much weight as _proofs_. They’re interesting as **historical reasoning** and as **philosophical frameworks**, but not as _demonstrations_ that a God exists.
In an academic or serious debate:

- A theist might use the _Five Ways_ as _suggestive_ — showing belief in God is _reasonable_, not _proven_.
- A skeptic or philosopher will quickly point out the logical gaps, circular reasoning, and dependence on outdated metaphysics.
- The discussion often shifts from _proof_ to _plausibility_ — whether God is a simpler or more meaningful explanation than a purely natural one.

### 1. **Argument from Motion**

> Everything in motion must be moved by something else; this leads to a First Mover, which is God.

**Best Rebuttal**: **Causality may not apply before time.**  
Aquinas assumes a linear chain of causes. But if time itself began with the universe, the idea of “before” the first motion doesn’t make sense — there’s no earlier state needing a cause.

**Alternative Rebuttal:**
 The assumption that “everything in motion must be moved by another” no longer fits modern physics. Objects move due to inertia unless acted upo n — not everything needs a mover.
### Example: A hockey puck on ice
	
You slide a hockey puck across smooth ice. It keeps moving even after you stop pushing it.

- **Aquinas’s view:** The puck must still be moved by something right now, otherwise it would stop — so there must be a continuous mover.
- **Modern physics:** According to Newton’s first law (inertia), an object in motion stays in motion unless acted upon by an external force. Once the puck is moving, it doesn’t _need_ anything to keep it moving — it only slows down because of friction.

**Other Rebuttals**:
- Even if there were a “first cause,” it doesn’t logically follow that it must be a conscious deity. It could be an impersonal physical event (like the Big Bang).
- Saying “God started motion” just pushes the problem back: who or what caused God to exist or act?


### 2. **Argument from Causation**

> Everything has a cause; nothing causes itself; therefore there must be a First Cause — God.

**Rebuttal:**

- This contradicts itself: if “everything must have a cause,” then God must too.
- If you say “God is the uncaused cause,” that’s special pleading — arbitrarily exempting God from the rule.
- Quantum physics shows uncaused events can occur (e.g., virtual particle fluctuations), so causation may not be universal.
- A “first cause” doesn’t have to be a mind or person — it could be a natural phenomenon.
    

---

### 3. **Argument from Contingency**

> Things exist but might not have existed; there must be a necessary being that always exists — God.

**Rebuttal:**

- The concept of a “necessary being” is abstract — there’s no evidence that existence requires one.
- The universe itself might be necessary, or eternal, or self-contained.
- “Necessity” here is defined philosophically, not empirically — it doesn’t prove anything exists beyond the natural world.

---

### 4. **Argument from Degree (Perfection)**

> Things vary in goodness, truth, nobility; there must be a perfect standard — God.

**Rebuttal:**

- This is based on a false assumption that degrees of quality imply an absolute maximum.
- We can compare “good” and “bad” without needing a perfect example (e.g., we know some colors are darker without postulating a “perfect black”).
- Morality and value judgments are human constructs, not evidence of a divine benchmark.

---

### 5. **Argument from Design (Teleology)**

> Natural things act toward ends; this order implies a designer — God.

**Rebuttal:**

- Evolution by natural selection explains apparent design without invoking intent.
- Order can arise naturally from physical laws (e.g., crystal formation, gravity organizing planets).
- “Design” is an interpretation, not evidence. We infer purpose because humans think in terms of purpose.
